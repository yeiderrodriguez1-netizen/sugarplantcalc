Theoretical background and formulas

Juice density at 20 °C (polynomial correlation)

\[\rho_{20} = 998.70 + 3.91679\,Bx + 0.0135108\,Bx^2 + 2.95998\times10^{-5}\,Bx^3\]

Temperature correction

\[\rho(t) = \rho_{20} - \left(0.24707\,\Delta t + 0.003169\,\Delta t^2\right), \quad \Delta t = t - 20\]

Baumé degrees

\[°B\acute{e} = 145 - \frac{145}{SG}\]

For syrup and molasses the same juice density model is applied with a product-specific correction factor, since their non-sugars and viscosity differ from diluted juice (Hugot).

Reference literature: E. Hugot, Handbook of Cane Sugar Engineering (density correlations for sugar solutions); Peter Rein, Cane Sugar Engineering; Meade–Chen, Cane Sugar Handbook (Baumé scale).

Frequently asked questions

Why does molasses density use a different factor than juice?

Molasses has a higher concentration of non-sugars (ash, gums) that raise its density beyond what the pure sucrose-water correlation for juice predicts; a correction factor of 1.010 is applied.

What are Baumé degrees and why are they used in a mill?

It is a specific gravity scale widely used in the field because it can be read directly with a hydrometer without calculating Brix; useful for quick control of milk of lime and syrups.

Why does the calculator warn the Brix is outside the typical range?

Each product (juice, syrup, molasses) has a normal concentration window in the process; a value outside that range usually means the wrong product was selected or there is a real process issue.

How does temperature affect measured density?

As temperature rises, the density of a sugar solution decreases due to thermal expansion; that is why any field hydrometer or refractometer must be corrected or read at the reference temperature (usually 20 °C).
