Theoretical background and formulas

Enzyme consumption

\[Enzyme_{g/h} = Juice_{flow}\,(t/h) \times Target_{dose}\,(ppm)\]

Dosing pump flow (diluted enzyme)

\[Q_{pump}\,(L/h) = \frac{Enzyme_{g/h}/\rho_{enzyme}}{1000 \times (Dilution_{\%}/100)}\]

A typical thermostable alpha-amylase works best between 75 °C and 98 °C, matching limed or clarified juice temperature; outside that range starch gelatinization is incomplete or the enzyme denatures (Eggleston, ISSCT).

Reference literature: Peter Rein, Cane Sugar Engineering (starch and its enzymatic control); E. Hugot, Handbook of Cane Sugar Engineering; Meade–Chen, Cane Sugar Handbook (starch and filterability).

Frequently asked questions

What starch level in juice is considered high?

Above 500 ppm is considered high starch, with risk of high viscosity in syrup and massecuites, and possible commercial sugar quality rejection due to turbidity.

What is the optimal temperature for dosing alpha-amylase?

The typical working range is 75–98 °C; below 75 °C starch does not fully gelatinise so the enzyme cannot access it, and above 98 °C there is a risk of thermal inactivation.

Where is alpha-amylase normally dosed in the process?

It is usually dosed into hot limed juice or at the entrance to clarification, where temperature is already in the enzyme's optimal range and there is enough residence time before evaporation.

What happens if residence time is very low even at the correct temperature?

With less than 5 minutes of contact, starch hydrolysis is incomplete even at optimal temperature; the calculator flags "very low residence time" in that case.
